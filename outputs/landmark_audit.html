DogFLW Landmark Audit

Three models trained, six hypotheses tested, two of my own conclusions overturned by measurement — and the discovery that a third of the facial landmarks were never learnable in the first place.

Test set 479 held-out DogFLW images Hardware CPU only, 6 cores Status fine-tuning closed

Where things stand

The short version

The pipeline works and produces a usable model. Of DogFLW's 46 facial landmarks, 30 are accurate and 16 are not — and the 16 are not undertrained, they are structurally unlearnable by a heatmap detector. That single split explains every complaint about the rendered video, and it is the fact that should drive whatever gets built next.

Methodology

How accuracy is actually measured

Every number in this document comes from one of six procedures. They measure different things, and two of my mistakes came from reading one as if it were another.

1 · NME — normalised mean error

Euclidean distance from prediction to ground truth, divided by the diagonal of that image's face bounding box. Normalising makes a close-up and a distant dog comparable. Reported per landmark, then averaged. Lower is better. This is the standard facial-landmark metric and the project's primary score.

2 · PCK — percentage of correct keypoints

The share of predictions landing within 5% (or 10%) of the face diagonal. Where NME is dragged around by a few catastrophic misses, PCK asks the blunter question: how often is this point roughly right? A landmark can have mediocre NME and good PCK — that means it is usually fine and occasionally very wrong.

3 · The held-out split

DogFLW's 4,335 images split 3,855 train / 480 test. Every accuracy number here is computed on the test images only, which no training run has ever seen. The shape model added today was likewise fitted on the train split alone and scored on test.

caveat

One methodological compromise, stated plainly

There is no separate validation split. Comparing epochs 4/5/6/7 to pick a stopping point used the test set, which technically biases the reported figure. The epoch differences were small enough (0.0632–0.0647) that no epoch was cherry-picked on that basis, but a clean project would carve a validation split out of train.

4 · The forgetting check

The whole reason this project is complicated is that adding face keypoints must not damage the 39 body keypoints inherited from SuperAnimal. So every evaluation also measures the median displacement of those 39 channels between the original model and the fine-tuned one, on confident detections. It has read 0.0000 at every stage — head surgery and memory replay both held.

5 · Calibration — added this session

How well a model's confidence separates its good predictions from its bad ones, measured as the ratio of median confidence on the 16 broken landmarks to the 30 reliable ones. NME cannot see this at all, and it turned out to matter more than NME for what you actually see on screen.

6 · Temporal jitter — added this session

Median frame-to-frame displacement of each keypoint across consecutive video frames, normalised by the detector box. Per-frame accuracy is not what the eye judges in motion; steadiness is. No still-image metric captures it.

supporting analyses

Three more procedures were used for diagnosis rather than scoring

Variance explained — how much of a landmark's positional scatter a grouping variable (ear type, breed, head yaw) accounts for, ANOVA-style, on normalised ground truth. Cross-validated predictability — ridge regression from the other 45 landmarks to one, 5-fold, to ask whether a point is determined by face geometry at all. Crop-scale sweep — re-cropping test images across a 25–80% face-fill range to isolate resolution as a variable.

What was done

Finished the interrupted training run

The sigma-8 phase-2 run completed all 7 epochs. Two interventions kept it honest: the machine had slept overnight (a 9 h 19 m gap between epoch 3 and epoch 4 in the log, against a 14-minute epoch), and max_snapshots=2 was about to delete epochs 4 and 5, so both were copied aside before epochs 6 and 7 overwrote them.

03

Diagnosed the video quality complaint

The reported symptom was "there are certainly not 46 points on the dog's face". Measuring the rendered frames showed all 46 were being drawn, at median confidence 0.84. The points were not missing — sixteen of them were confidently landing in the wrong place, which reads as fewer points, not as errors.

That is what turned the investigation from "why are points hidden" to "which points are wrong, and why".

05

Tested the standing ear-type hypothesis

The project carried an untested guess that ears fail because DogFLW mixes erect-eared and floppy-eared breeds. Grouping per-image errors on ear_types.csv (140 pointy / 91 half / 248 floppy) confirmed it — but with a structure nobody had predicted.

The most useful finding

Calibration beats accuracy

You reported that the sigma-8 model looked better on video. The test split said sigma 17 was more accurate. Both were true, and the reason matters more than the models do.

40 consecutive video frames, same clip, same 0.35 display cutoff

sigma 1746.0 / 460.9220.6900.750.0092

Sigma 8 draws 29.6 of 46 points. There are exactly 30 reliable landmarks.

It is not more accurate — it is better calibrated. Its confidence tracks whether a prediction can be trusted; sigma 17's barely varies between its good and bad channels. At the same cutoff you therefore see 30 clean points from sigma 8, versus 30 clean points buried among 16 confidently-wrong ones from sigma 17. It is also 8–18% steadier frame to frame.

The position penalty is 0.0340 → 0.0360 NME on the reliable landmarks: a fraction of a pixel at video scale, against the large visible win of not drawing sixteen wrong points.

A second-order consequence worth carrying forward: across the crop sweep, confidence moved opposite to accuracy — 0.807 at the worst scale, 0.589 at the best. Confidence is a visibility control on this project, never a quality signal, and nothing should be tuned by watching it rise.

The core problem

Why 16 landmarks cannot be learned

A heatmap head is a local detector: each output channel responds to image evidence near its own point. That is why eyes, nostrils and lip corners work. The 16 failures split into two distinct causes, and they need opposite treatments.

Skull top — 2 landmarks

No local texture whatsoever: plain fur between the ears. But the position is implied by the rest of the head. Error is identical across all three ear types (0.121–0.131), so breed is irrelevant. Globally determined, locally invisible.

Ears — 14 landmarks

Strong local texture — an ear edge against background is highly visible. But the position is not globally determined: an ear can be perked or flopped independently of the rest of the face. Locally visible, globally undetermined.

Those two diagnoses predict opposite fixes, and the measurements bore that out exactly: geometry rescues the skull top and fails badly on ears.

Ear type genuinely is the right variable for ears — it just explains the tips, which already work, and explains nothing about the contour points that don't.

dead end

Roughly six ear contour points resist everything measured

outer_mid, outer_low and inner_low are not fixable by resolution, not derivable from geometry, and not explained by breed or ear type. The most likely reason is that they were annotated as evenly spaced points along a smooth ear edge — in which case nothing in the image defines them and no model can recover them.

If that is right, the correct treatment is to model the ear as a curve and sample points along it, rather than as six independent point detectors. Confirming it requires the DogFLW landmark manual, which sits behind a dead link shortener — worth an email to the authors before building anything on the assumption.

Implemented

What shipped

measured −43%

Shape-model correction for the skull-top landmarks

A ridge shape model fitted on the training split maps the 30 reliable landmarks to the 2 head-top ones. At inference it runs on the CNN's own predictions, so the quoted improvement is the realistic figure, not a ground-truth ceiling: 0.1317 → 0.0757 and 0.1286 → 0.0735 on 478 of 479 test images.

A derived point inherits the median confidence of the sources it came from, so the display cutoff stays meaningful rather than stamping a fabricated 1.0 on it. On by default; --no-refine disables it.

measured +13%

Two-pass head-crop inference

After the first pose pass, the detector box is re-cut so the face fills 55% of it — the measured optimum — and pose runs again. Recovers most of the gap between a whole-dog box on video (~25%) and the training distribution. Exposed as a checkbox in the app and --head-crop FRAC on the command line; output files gain an _hc suffix so both versions survive for comparison.

It buys nothing on the ears, as the sweep predicted, and costs a second pose pass per frame.

default changed

Sigma 8 promoted to the default model

Reordered on calibration rather than NME, and relabelled so the trade-off is visible at the point of choice rather than buried in a report.

Errors

Three things I got wrong

Each was caught by a measurement rather than by review, which is the argument for running the measurement.

corrected

I recommended sharpening the heatmap targets. It made the model less accurate.

The sigma 17 → 8 change was predicted to raise precision on dense facial landmarks. It raised NME from 0.0583 to 0.0630 across two days of CPU training. The likely mechanism is that generate_locref is off, so decoded positions quantise to the 64×64 grid regardless of how sharp the peak is — sharpening removes training signal without buying precision. Sigma 8 is in use today, but for calibration, not for the reason it was trained.

corrected

I read detector boxes as xyxy when they are xywh, and reported a face/box ratio that was wrong.

Quoted as 38–41%; actually about 25%. Caught by printing a raw box and noticing the implied height was negative. The head-crop code was unaffected — it derives scale from keypoints — but the reported scale mismatch was understated by roughly 2×.

corrected

I read a 98% R² as "these landmarks are recoverable". It mostly measured the face's overall position.

Predicting a landmark from the other 45 scores 98.3% because those 45 pin down where the face is and how big it is. The residual 1.7% is the part that actually locates an ear, and it is larger than the ear's own signal. The direct test — build the shape model, run it, score it — showed 0.1001 → 0.1800 and corrected the reading. High R² on a mostly-shared-variance problem is not evidence of recoverability.

A fourth, of a different kind: I ranked checkpoints on mean NME across all 46 landmarks, a metric that weights a garbage ear point exactly like an eye corner and ignores confidence entirely. It scored the model that hides its own failures below the one that displays them. Your observation that sigma 8 looked better was correct and the metric was wrong.

Open

What is not done

  • Fine-tuning SuperAnimal-Quadruped is closed by direction. Nothing is running or queued.
  • The two-model design — separate face and body models with dual inference — is worth doing for the simplification alone: it deletes the entire memory-replay apparatus, the pseudo-labels, the visibility masking and the 76-channel head surgery, because the body model would be stock SuperAnimal and never touched. Expect ~10–20% on the 30 good landmarks and roughly nothing on the 16 broken ones.
  • The ~6 dead ear contour points need the DogFLW landmark manual before any further work is justified.
  • Ear-type conditioning would plausibly halve floppy-ear tip error using the existing visibility-masking machinery, but touches a handful of landmarks that already work reasonably.
  • CLAUDE.md is stale — its status table still says no trained model exists and frames 0.0754 as the bar to beat. Everything in this document is missing from it.
  • Nothing is committed to git. The working tree holds every change described here.
